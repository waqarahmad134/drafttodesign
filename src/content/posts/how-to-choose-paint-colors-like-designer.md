Picking paint looks simple until you are staring at 4,000 swatches that all look like the same gray. Designers make it look easy because they never pick a color, they pick a color for a specific room, a specific light, and a specific feeling. This guide covers the method they use: undertones, light, the 60-30-10 rule, professional paint testing, and room-by-room palettes. ## Step 1: Read the undertone, not just the color

Every paint color hides a second color beneath the first. Two "neutral grays" can be completely different: one leans blue and feels crisp, the other leans brown and feels warm and cozy. That hidden color is the undertone, and it decides whether a color harmonizes or clashes with your floors and fixed finishes.

The designer test takes five seconds. Hold the swatch next to a sheet of white paper and the undertone jumps out: green, blue, purple, or brown. Then check your fixed finishes, flooring, countertops, tile. Paint is cheapest; the finishes are not, so the undertone must work with them.

Three undertone rules:

- **Match fixed finishes first.** Warm oak floors want warm-leaning walls; cool concrete wants cool-leaning walls.
- **Two or three undertone families per room max.** More than that feels scattered.
- **Learn "greige."** Gray plus beige carries a warm undertone that plays well with wood floors and white trim, which is why designers specify it constantly.

## Step 2: Use the 60-30-10 rule

The 60-30-10 rule is the proportion formula behind nearly every room you have admired. About 60 percent of the room is the dominant color (usually walls), 30 percent is secondary (sofa, curtains, bedding), 10 percent is accent (pillows, art, one chair). The balance is the point: enough unity to feel calm, enough contrast to feel alive.

In practice: soft warm white walls take the 60, a sage green sofa and drapery carry the 30, and the 10 is where you get brave, terracotta cushions, brass lamps, a black side table. If a room feels boring, the fix is almost never a new wall color. It is a bigger, braver 10 percent.

The exception is the dark, moody room, which flips the formula. A deep charcoal or navy wall (the 60), creamy furniture (the 30), wood and brass accents (the 10) is the designer recipe for dens and bedrooms. Never split 50-50 between two strong colors.

## Step 3: Check the LRV number

Every serious paint brand publishes a Light Reflectance Value, or LRV. It runs from 0 (pure black) to 100 (pure white) and tells you exactly how light or dark a color will feel on the wall. It is the most honest number in paint.

The ranges worth memorizing:

- **70 to 100:** whites and near-whites. Ceilings, small rooms, spaces that need every photon.
- **45 to 70:** light to medium. The safe zone for most living spaces.
- **25 to 45:** medium to dark. Accent walls, dining rooms, rooms with generous windows.
- **Below 25:** deep and dramatic. One wall, one small room, or a tall ceiling.

Why it matters more than the swatch: two colors that look similar on paper can be 15 LRV points apart, and on the wall that gap reads as a full shade of darkness. ## Step 4: Work with your light, not against it

The same gallon of paint is four different colors in four different rooms. Light is the second-most important color decision in every room, after the paint itself.

**North-facing rooms** get cool, indirect light all day and never see direct sun. Colors look truer to the swatch here, but warm colors counter the cool cast: creams, warm whites, warm grays, muted greens. Cool grays in a north room can read cold and flat.

**South-facing rooms** get warm, intense light most of the day. Whites can look yellowish, and bold colors get amplified, that red you loved in the store may shout in a south room. Cool-leaning colors balance the warmth, and you can handle deeper colors because the light will not swallow them.

**East and west rooms** are split personalities. East is bright and warm in the morning, cool and dim by evening; west is the reverse, dim morning and hot orange afternoon. Test at three times of day: morning, noon, and evening light are effectively three different products.

Two factors people forget. **Bulb color** shifts everything: daylight LEDs (5000K and up) read cooler and harsher; warm LEDs (2700K) flatter warm colors but can make cool blues muddy. **Sheen** matters too: higher sheen looks slightly darker and richer. Use flat or eggshell on walls, satin on trim, semi-gloss on doors.

## Step 5: Make colors flow room to room

One undertone family running through the home, with room-by-room variation in depth, is the whole-house color story that keeps everything feeling calm instead of chaotic.

The reliable version:

- **Hallways:** your lightest, most neutral color. The glue between rooms.
- **Living areas:** the 60-30-10 palette built off that neutral.
- **Private rooms:** the same undertone family, deeper. A warm greige living room and a deeper warm taupe bedroom feel connected.
- **Repeating accents:** pick one accent color and echo it in every room, a plant, a piece of art, a throw. The cheapest flow trick there is.

In open-plan homes, use one wall color throughout and let furniture define zones. Breaking an open plan into three wall colors chops it into a smaller-looking space. For definition, use the 10 percent, a rug, an art wall, a painted island, not more wall colors.

## Step 6: Test paint like a professional

This is the step almost everyone skips, and the one that saves almost everyone from a $600 repaint. Never choose from a two-inch swatch, and never trust store lighting.

1. **Buy sample quarts** of your two or three finalists, usually under $10 each.
2. **Paint large test boards, not the wall.** Two 2-by-2-foot foam boards, two coats each. Boards move around the room and leave no test patches behind.
3. **Tape them up** and view them in morning, noon, and evening light, with your normal lamps on at night.
4. **Look at them next to your trim, flooring, and biggest furniture piece.** Colors only exist in context.
5. **Live with them 48 hours.** Colors you love on day one can irritate you by day two.

Test in the **actual finish** you will use, since flat, eggshell, and satin of the same color are visibly different. Paint also dries about one shade darker and cooler than it looks wet, so do not judge a fresh sample until it dries overnight.

## Room-by-room starting palettes

**Bedrooms** want calm: soft warm whites, warm greiges, muted sage, dusty blue. In darker rooms, deep charcoal or olive makes a sleep-friendly mood. Skip high-saturation colors, they measurably hurt restfulness.

**Living rooms** want warmth and flexibility: warm whites, greiges, soft taupes. The accent layer does the heavy lifting. If your furniture is cool-toned, a warm-leaning wall is almost mandatory.

**Kitchens** read best in clean, light colors that bounce task light: warm whites, pale greiges. White cabinets plus a colored island (deep blue, black, forest green) is the classic designer move.

**Bathrooms** can take more drama than people expect. Small powder rooms are where designers go bold: deep navy or emerald with brass fixtures is a flawless powder-room move.

**Exteriors** follow different physics: sun washes everything out, so go one shade darker and slightly more saturated than you think. The front door is the accent playground, charcoal, navy, or classic red against a light body never miss. ## The 7 most common paint mistakes

1. **Choosing from the swatch only.** Two-inch chips lie. Go to sample boards.
2. **Ignoring fixed-finish undertones.** The gray clashes with your beige tile because of a blue undertone, not because of the gray.
3. **Going too light in a dark room.** Light paint does not add light where there are no windows, it just reads flat.
4. **Painting every room a different color.** Flow beats variety, especially in open plans.
5. **Matching instead of coordinating.** Pillows need to share the undertone family, not match the wall exactly.
6. **Forgetting the ceiling.** Slightly lighter than the walls makes low ceilings feel taller.
7. **Testing under store lighting only.** Store fluorescents are not your home.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many colors should a whole house have?

Three to five wall colors, all sharing one undertone family. One main neutral for most walls, one or two supporting colors for private rooms, accents handled through furniture and art.

### Should all my trim be white?

White trim is the safest choice in about 90 percent of homes. The modern alternative is painting trim the same color as the walls in a higher sheen, which makes small rooms feel larger. Worst option: cream trim against cool gray walls, a classic undertone clash.

### What is the best white paint color?

Warm, south-facing rooms often take a cooler white; north-facing rooms take a warmer one. Test two or three in your actual space.

### How much does it cost to repaint a room?

A professional repaint of an average 12-by-12-foot bedroom runs roughly $300 to $800 for labor plus materials in most US markets. A whole-house interior repaint typically falls between $3,500 and $8,000 depending on size, region, and condition.

### Can I mix warm and cool colors in one room?

Yes, but one side has to lead. Warm wood floors with cool blue walls is a classic pairing, and it works because the undertones stay consistent within each element. Pick one temperature as the room's default and use the other as the 10 percent accent.
