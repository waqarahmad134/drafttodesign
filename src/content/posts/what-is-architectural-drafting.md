Every building you have ever walked into started as a set of lines on a page. Before a single wall goes up, someone has to translate an idea, "a three-bedroom house with a big open kitchen," into precise, buildable instructions a contractor can follow without guessing. That translation is architectural drafting.

If you are planning a new build, a renovation, or an addition, you will hear the words "plans," "elevations," and "sections" almost immediately. This guide explains what architectural drafting actually is, what each core drawing type shows, how the drawings work together, and how to get a drawing set for your own project.

## What architectural drafting is, in plain language

Architectural drafting is the craft of producing the technical drawings that describe a building accurately enough to be built, priced, and approved. Think of it as the instruction manual for construction. Where a sketch captures an idea, a drafted set captures every measurement, material decision, and spatial relationship a builder needs to execute it.

Historically, drafters worked by hand on drawing boards with pencils, T-squares, and ink. Today, nearly all professional drafting is done with CAD software (computer-aided design) or BIM tools, which produce drawings that are precise to a fraction of an inch and easy to revise. The tools changed; the purpose did not: remove ambiguity so that what gets built matches what was planned.

Drafting sits between design and construction. A designer decides what the space should feel like; the drafter turns that into dimensions, wall types, door swings, ceiling heights, and roof geometry.

## The three core drawing types

Almost every residential drawing set is built around three views of the same building. Once you understand these three, the rest of the set starts making sense.

### Floor plans: the building viewed from above

A floor plan is what you see if you imagine slicing the building horizontally about four feet above the floor and looking straight down. Walls appear as thick parallel lines, doors show as openings with swing arcs, and windows appear as gaps in the walls. Rooms are labeled with names and dimensions, and fixtures like sinks, toilets, stoves, and bathtubs are drawn in.

Floor plans carry the most information per sheet. They show:

- Room sizes and overall building dimensions
- Wall thicknesses and which walls are structural
- Door and window sizes and locations
- The layout of kitchens, bathrooms, and utility areas
- Traffic flow, how you move from the entry to the bedrooms, for example

Most homes are drawn at a scale of 1/4 inch = 1 foot, so a 20-foot living room spans 5 inches on the sheet. For larger homes, 1/8 inch = 1 foot is sometimes used.

### Elevations: the building viewed straight on

An elevation shows one face of the building as if you were standing directly in front of it, with no perspective distortion. There are typically four: front, rear, and both sides. While a floor plan tells you how space is organized horizontally, elevations tell you how the building looks and how tall it is.

Elevations show:

- Exterior wall materials (brick, siding, stucco) and how they transition
- Window and door styles and their heights from the ground
- Roof shape, slope, and overhangs
- Porches, balconies, decks, and other attached elements

Because elevations are drawn flat, they are also what rendering artists and material suppliers work from. If you have ever approved a 3D exterior render, it was almost certainly built from the elevations paired with the floor plan.

### Sections: the building cut open

A section drawing slices the building vertically and shows what the cut reveals: ceiling heights, floor-to-floor structure, stair geometry, roof framing, and how interior and exterior meet.

Sections are where hidden complexity lives. A staircase that looks simple on a floor plan shows its full logic in section: tread depth, riser height, headroom clearance, and how the stair lands on each floor. Even a simple two-story home usually needs at least one or two.

## What a complete drawing set includes

Plans, elevations, and sections are the core, but a permit-ready or contractor-ready set usually includes several more sheets. A typical residential set ordered from a [drafting service](/services/architectural-drafting/) includes:

- **Cover sheet** with project information, drawing index, and general notes
- **Site plan** showing the building footprint on the lot, setbacks, and driveways
- **Floor plans** for every level, including foundation or basement
- **Elevations** for all four sides
- **Building sections**, typically one longitudinal and one transverse
- **Roof plan** showing slopes, ridges, and drainage
- **Electrical plan** with outlets, switches, and lighting
- **Door and window schedules** listing every opening with sizes and types
- **Details and notes** covering wall assemblies and special conditions

Not every project needs all ten. A small interior remodel might only need floor plans, an electrical plan, and a few details. A new custom home needs the full set. A good drafter scopes the set to the project instead of selling you sheets you will never use.

## Scales and symbols: how to read the sheets

Drawing sets follow conventions so that any contractor can read any set. A few basics help you read your own plans with confidence:

- **Dimension lines** show exact measurements in feet and inches. Trust the written dimension over measuring the drawing itself.
- **Hatch patterns** indicate materials: diagonal lines often mean brick, dots can mean concrete.
- **Callouts** like "see detail A/5" point you from one sheet to the drawing that shows the detail at larger scale.

The most common residential scales are 1/4 inch = 1 foot for plans and elevations, 3/4 inch = 1 foot for enlarged details, and 1 inch = 1 foot for construction details.

## Drafting vs. architecture vs. design

These terms get mixed up constantly, so here is the clean version:

- **Architects** design the building conceptually and, where required, stamp drawings for permits. They carry liability for structural and code decisions.
- **Drafters** produce the technical drawings themselves, often working from an architect's concept, a designer's sketch, or a homeowner's marked-up plan.
- **Interior designers** focus on the look, feel, and function of interior spaces: finishes, furniture, lighting mood.
- **3D rendering artists** turn drafted drawings into photorealistic visuals used for marketing, client approval, and pre-construction visualization.

In practice, these roles overlap. Many residential projects do not need a full architect; a skilled drafter working with the homeowner's ideas (and an engineer where structure is involved) is the standard path for most custom homes, additions, and ADUs. ## How long drafting takes and what it costs

Timelines depend on project size and how decisive the client is. As rough 2026 benchmarks from remote drafting services:

- **Single-room remodel or small addition:** 1 to 2 weeks, $400 to $900
- **Full single-family home (1,500 to 3,000 sq ft):** 3 to 5 weeks, $1,200 to $3,000
- **Large custom home (3,000+ sq ft):** 5 to 8 weeks, $3,000 to $7,000+

The biggest schedule risk is revisions. Every round of "actually, let's move the kitchen" redraws multiple sheets, so finalizing your layout early saves both time and money.

## How to prepare before you hire a drafter

You will get a better set, faster and cheaper, if you arrive prepared:

1. **Know your scope.** New build, addition, or remodel? Which rooms are affected?
2. **Gather references.** Photos of homes you like or a rough hand sketch all help enormously.
3. **List must-haves.** Bedrooms, baths, garage bays, office, laundry location, approximate square footage.
4. **Check local requirements.** Some jurisdictions require an architect's stamp for new builds; many allow drafters for residential work. A quick call to your building department answers this.
5. **Have site info ready.** Lot dimensions and any survey you have. The site plan depends on it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need an architect, or is a drafter enough?

For most residential projects, a drafter is enough. Architects become necessary when local code requires a licensed stamp, when the structure is complex, or when you want full design services. For straightforward custom homes, additions, and remodels, a drafting service plus a structural engineer where needed is the standard, cost-effective path.

### What file formats will I receive?

You should receive PDF sheets for printing and review, plus the native CAD files (usually DWG) so future revisions stay easy. PDFs are what contractors and permit offices use; DWG files are what future drafters and 3D artists need.

### Can a drafter work from my hand sketch or an old blueprint?

Yes. A clear hand sketch with room labels and approximate dimensions is a fine starting point. Old blueprints can be redrawn into clean CAD files, which is useful when remodeling and the original drawings are outdated or damaged.

### How many revision rounds are normal?

Two to three revision rounds are typical in a fixed-price package: one after the initial draft for major layout changes, and one or two for refinements. Lock in room positions early, because late structural layout changes cost the most.

### What is the difference between 2D floor plans and 3D floor plans?

2D floor plans are the technical top-down drawings used for construction. 3D floor plans are perspective visuals that show the same layout with walls, furniture, and finishes rendered in three dimensions, useful for understanding how a space will feel. If you need the technical set, start with [2D floor plans](/services/2d-floor-plans/); if you want presentation visuals, 3D comes after.

### Will my drawing set be enough for a building permit?

Usually yes for residential work, provided the set includes everything your jurisdiction requires. Requirements vary by city and county, so confirm the checklist with your building department before finalizing.
