Walk down any American street and you will read its history in the houses: the deep front porch with exposed rafters, the symmetrical brick facade with a centered front door, the flat roof with walls of glass. Craftsman, Colonial and Modern are the three home styles most buyers keep returning to, and the choice goes far beyond looks. Each style carries a typical floor plan, a typical relationship to the lot, and a typical cost profile. Pick the wrong one, and you will fight the house every day.

This guide compares the three side by side: defining features, interior layouts, costs, and which households each one suits.

## Craftsman: the honest, hand-built home

The Craftsman movement began in the early 1900s as a reaction against mass-produced Victorian ornament. Inspired by the Arts and Crafts movement, Craftsman homes celebrate honest materials and visible workmanship: wood, stone and brick used plainly, joints and beams left exposed rather than hidden.

### What it looks like from the street

A classic Craftsman bungalow is one to one and a half stories with a low-pitched gable roof, wide eaves and exposed rafter tails. The signature feature is the deep front porch, usually 8 to 10 feet, supported by tapered columns on stone or brick piers. Siding runs horizontal wood, shingle or stucco in earth tones like sage, brown and warm gray.

### How it feels inside

Craftsman interiors are cozy by design. Ceilings run 8 to 9 feet. The living room often centers on a stone or brick fireplace flanked by built-in bookcases, and built-ins show up everywhere: window seats, inglenook benches, dining room china cabinets. Classic plans run 1,200 to 2,200 square feet, with bedrooms down a short hallway and rooms connected by wide cased openings rather than full open plans, so you get flow without noise carrying through the whole house.

### What it costs

Craftsman detailing is labor-intensive, and labor is the expensive part. Custom woodwork, built-ins, tapered columns and stone accents typically push new construction 10 to 15 percent above a comparable modern box, generally $200 to $280 per square foot in Texas and Florida depending on the millwork. Remodeling one means matching old wood trim and plaster details, so budget 15 to 20 percent over a standard renovation.

## Colonial: the symmetrical, formal classic

Colonial is the oldest of the three styles, with roots in the 1600s. The Colonial Revival and Georgian forms most buyers know were standardized in the early 20th century and became the default suburban home across the Northeast, Midwest and Mid-Atlantic.

### What it looks like from the street

Symmetry is the whole game. The front door sits dead center, flanked by evenly spaced windows, typically five across upstairs and four around the door on the first floor. The roof is steeply pitched with side gables, often with a chimney at each end. Materials are brick, clapboard or stone, with louvered shutters in a contrasting color framing the windows.

### How it feels inside

Colonial floor plans are formal and predictable, which many buyers consider a feature. A center hall runs front to back, living room on one side, dining room on the other, kitchen at the back. Upstairs holds three to four bedrooms around a landing. Ceilings run 8 to 9 feet on the first floor, 8 feet on the second. Want open-concept living? Expect structural work: the load-bearing walls defining the center-hall plan need engineered beams to come down, and that is where remodel budgets surprise people.

### What it costs

Colonials are straightforward to build: the rectangular footprint and simple roof keep framing costs down, typically $180 to $250 per square foot in the primary markets. Brick facades and two-story foyers push toward the upper end. Where renovations bite is systems: many were built before modern insulation, electrical and plumbing standards, so a gut remodel can run $120 to $200 per square foot once you open the walls.

## Modern: the light-filled, open-plan house

Modern architecture in America traces to the mid-century International Style and the postwar ranch house, evolving into what builders sell today: clean lines, large glass openings and open floor plans. "Modern" is the loosest of the three categories, but the family resemblance is unmistakable.

### What it looks like from the street

Low-pitched or flat roofs, horizontal lines, walls of glass. Materials mix freely: stucco with wood slats, metal panels with stone veneer. The front facade often hides the entry, with the garage or a screen wall facing the street and living spaces opening to the backyard.

### How it feels inside

This is the open plan in its purest form. Kitchen, dining and living flow into one large space, often under 10 to 12 foot ceilings, with big windows bringing abundant light and, in Texas or Florida, more heat gain. Interiors rely on the shell for character: ceiling planes, storage walls, carefully placed glazing. Fewer load-bearing interior walls also make future remodels easy.

### What it costs

Modern construction varies more than the other two styles because it covers everything from a simple stucco box to a custom glass pavilion. Production-style builds run $190 to $260 per square foot, while architect-driven custom modern can reach $350 to $500 per square foot. The hidden cost driver is glass: large window and sliding-door packages can add $40,000 to $80,000 over a conventional schedule. Flat and low-slope roofs also demand careful drainage detailing, so do not economize on the roofer.

| Feature | Craftsman | Colonial | Modern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical footprint | 1,200 to 2,200 sq ft | 1,800 to 3,200 sq ft | 1,500 to 3,500+ sq ft |
| Stories | 1 to 1.5 | 2 (sometimes 2.5) | 1 to 2 |
| Roof | Low-pitch gable, exposed rafters | Steep side gable | Flat or low-slope |
| Signature exterior | Deep front porch | Symmetrical facade, shutters | Walls of glass, mixed materials |

| Remodel flexibility | Moderate | Low (load-bearing walls) | High |
| New build cost per sq ft | $200 to $280 | $180 to $250 | $190 to $260+ |

## How to choose the right style for your situation

**Pick Craftsman if** you love character and craftsmanship, your lot is modest, and you are comfortable with a cozier, more compartmentalized interior. It suits neighborhoods with design review boards that favor traditional styles and holds value well in historic districts. Budget for quality millwork, because cheap details look wrong immediately.

**Pick Colonial if** you want maximum house for the money on a standard suburban lot and you prefer defined rooms over open plans. Colonials resell reliably in established Eastern and Midwestern markets, so get a thorough inspection on older ones and budget for systems upgrades.

**Pick Modern if** you live for natural light and indoor-outdoor living, and your climate or lot supports big glass without punishing energy bills. It is the strongest choice for challenging lots, narrow, sloped or oddly shaped parcels where a flexible plan solves problems a rigid Colonial footprint cannot.

**Think about the neighborhood.** A modern glass box on a street of 1920s Colonials can hurt resale no matter how well it is built, and an HOA may simply forbid it. Check deed restrictions and design guidelines before you fall in love with a style. Buyers also overpay to wedge a style onto the wrong lot, a sprawling Colonial on a narrow infill parcel, when an [exterior design plan](/services/exterior-design-landscaping/) would have shown the mismatch on day one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which home style has the best resale value?

It depends on the market. Colonials dominate in the Northeast and Midwest because buyers there expect them. Craftsman homes command premiums in historic, walkable neighborhoods like those in Portland, Denver and Austin. Modern homes sell fastest in markets with younger buyers and mild climates, such as Los Angeles, Phoenix and Miami. The consistent rule: the best-reselling style is the one that matches the neighborhood.

### Is it cheaper to build a modern home than a traditional one?

Not automatically. A simple modern box with stucco cladding can cost less than a brick Colonial or a millwork-heavy Craftsman, but high-end modern finishes, steel windows and flat-roof detailing push costs well past traditional construction. Get line-item bids on your actual drawings rather than trusting style-level averages. Our [pricing page](/pricing/) shows what design drawings cost so you can budget the planning phase accurately.

### Can I mix styles, like a modern interior in a Colonial exterior?

Yes, it is one of the most common remodel requests we see. Keeping a traditional exterior while opening the interior is very achievable, but load-bearing walls need structural engineering to come out. Plan on $15,000 to $40,000 in structural work alone for a serious first-floor opening in a two-story Colonial, plus finish costs.

### Do home styles affect insurance or energy costs?

They can. Flat or low-slope modern roofs may carry higher premiums in hail and hurricane zones like Texas and Florida. Colonials with old wiring and plumbing cost more to insure until systems are updated. On energy, big modern glass areas raise cooling loads in hot climates unless you specify high-performance glazing, while the smaller windows of Craftsman and Colonial homes are naturally easier to cool.

### Which style works best on a narrow lot?

Modern, usually. Its flexible geometry and ability to pull light from above through clerestories, skylights and courtyards make it the most adaptable to narrow or irregular parcels. A Colonial's rigid symmetrical footprint is the hardest to fit on a tight lot.

## The bottom line

Craftsman gives you warmth and craft, Colonial gives you formality and value, Modern gives you light and flexibility. The right style is the one that fits your lot, your budget, your household and your street. Decide those constraints first, then let the style follow.
