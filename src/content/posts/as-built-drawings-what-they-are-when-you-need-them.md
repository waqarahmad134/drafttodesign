You are about to remodel a kitchen, add a room, or sell a house, and someone asks for the as-built drawings. If you have never heard the term, it sounds like extra paperwork. In practice, it is one of the most useful documents a homeowner can own: a measured record of your home exactly as it stands today, not as it was drawn years ago. Original plans show intent. As-built drawings show reality. Walls move during construction, owners add closets, a remodel shifts a doorway by a foot, and none of that shows up on old blueprints. An as-built set closes that gap and protects every decision that follows, from cabinet sizes to contractor pricing.

## What an as-built drawing actually is

An as-built drawing is a scaled, dimensioned drawing made from field measurements of an existing building. A drafter or surveyor visits the property, measures every room, wall thickness, door, window, ceiling height, and fixed fixture, then draws those measurements to scale, usually at 1/4 inch equals 1 foot for residential plans. The result is a base drawing that everyone on a project can trust: you, your designer, your contractor, and, where required, your architect or engineer. It answers questions guesswork cannot, such as whether a standard 36 inch vanity will fit between two walls. As-built drawings are also called existing-conditions drawings or measured drawings, but the job is the same: document what is there, accurately, before anything changes.

## What a complete as-built set usually includes

A basic residential set centers on floor plans, one per level, with overall and room dimensions, wall thicknesses, door and window sizes and locations, stairs, and fixed fixtures such as tubs, toilets, counters, and built-in cabinets. Ceiling heights are noted room by room, because a plan view alone never tells you how tall a space feels.

Depending on the project, a set may also include exterior elevations, a roof plan, a reflected ceiling plan, and a site plan showing the footprint, driveways, and setbacks. Outlets, switches, and panel locations are often marked, as are visible plumbing and HVAC elements. Underground pipes and wiring inside walls are not shown unless they were exposed and measured, and any good drafter will say so plainly rather than guess. For a remodel, the most valuable sheets are the dimensioned plans and the elevations of walls that will change, since cabinets are ordered to the eighth of an inch and a two inch error can stall an installation for weeks.

## As-built vs original blueprints vs proposed plans

These three document types get confused constantly, and using the wrong one causes real problems.

Original blueprints show what was supposed to be built. They may be decades old, they may not reflect changes made during construction, and they almost never reflect later remodels. They are a helpful starting point, but they are not proof of current conditions. As-built drawings show what exists right now, measured on site, so they capture the moved wall, the added closet, and the window replaced with a smaller one. Proposed plans show what will exist after the work, drawn on top of the as-built base so new walls and fixtures are positioned against real dimensions. If a contractor prices a job from a plan drawn over an unverified old blueprint, the price is a guess. If it is drawn over a measured as-built, the price has a foundation. In short: original plans are history, as-builts are fact, and proposed plans are the future built on that fact.

## When you actually need as-built drawings

You need a measured set whenever accuracy matters more than a rough sketch, which covers most serious projects.

Remodels and additions come first. Any project that moves walls, changes a kitchen or bathroom layout, or adds square footage should start with as-builts, so contractors bid accurately and change orders drop sharply.

Permit and design review work is second. Architects and engineers routinely require existing-conditions drawings before they prepare their own plans, because their work must fit the real building. DraftToDesign provides the measured drawing set itself; permit preparation and submission, where a licensed professional is required, is handled by the architect or engineer on the project. The as-built set is what makes their job possible.

Buying, selling, and leasing is third. Buyers, appraisers, and tenants often want verified square footage and a reliable layout rather than a marketing floor plan. A measured set settles arguments about room sizes before they become deal problems. Insurance claims and damage repair are fourth: after fire, flood, or storm damage, an as-built record documents what existed and gives adjusters and contractors a shared reference. Historic homes and rental properties round out the list, since older homes almost never match their original plans and owners need a reliable record between tenants.

## When you probably do not need one

Not every job justifies a full measured set. Painting, flooring replacement in kind, and swapping fixtures in the same location can usually proceed from a simple sketch and a few tape measurements. A full set is still useful as a long term record, but it is not always required.

## How as-built drawings are made

A typical residential project follows five steps. First comes the site visit: the measurer walks the property, photographs each room, notes ceiling heights, and records anything unusual, such as sloped ceilings. Expect one to three hours for an average single family home.

Second comes measurement, room by room, with a laser distance meter and a tape, plus diagonal checks to catch out of square walls. Larger or highly detailed projects may use a 3D laser scan, which captures millions of points that the drafter traces. Scanning costs more, but it is faster on site and more precise for complex buildings. Third comes drafting in CAD software, fourth comes quality control, where room dimensions are cross checked against overall dimensions and photos are reviewed to catch missed elements such as a panel or a low beam. Fifth comes delivery, usually as PDF plans plus editable CAD files such as DWG or DXF. Ask for both, since CAD files let your designer build proposed plans directly on top of the as-built base without redrawing anything.

## What as-built drawings cost and how long they take

Cost depends on size, complexity, number of levels, and whether laser scanning is used. As a realistic US guide for 2026, a small condo under 1,000 square feet typically runs 250 to 500 dollars, an average single family home of 1,500 to 2,500 square feet runs 500 to 1,200 dollars, and larger homes and commercial spaces run 1,200 to 2,500 dollars or more, with laser scanned projects at the higher end.

Turnaround for a standard residential set is usually 3 to 7 business days after the site visit. Laser scanned or very large projects may take 1 to 2 weeks. Remote drafting, where you supply your own measurements and photos, can be faster and cheaper, but it is only as accurate as the measurements you provide. To compare quotes fairly, ask every firm the same questions: does the price include all levels, are elevations included, will you receive CAD files as well as PDFs, and how many revision rounds are included? The cheapest quote that delivers only a PDF is often the most expensive option once design work begins.

## How to prepare for a measurement visit

Clear access to every room, closet, attic hatch, and utility area, and unlock gates and outbuildings in scope. Gather old plans, a prior survey, and notes about past remodels: they do not replace measurement, but they help the measurer spot changes faster. Mention known quirks in advance, such as a room that was once a garage. Finally, confirm the scope in writing: which levels are included and what file formats you will receive.

## Common mistakes to avoid

The biggest mistake is building a design on old blueprints without verifying them. A six inch difference can break a kitchen layout or a stair design, usually discovered after materials are ordered. The second mistake is accepting an unmeasured marketing plan as a construction base: real estate plans are often drawn without dimensions and are not reliable for pricing. The third mistake is skipping ceiling heights, wall thicknesses, and window sill heights, which forces your designer to guess, and guesses become change orders. The fourth mistake is losing the files, so store your PDFs and CAD files with your other house records, with a backup copy.

## Frequently asked questions

### How accurate are as-built drawings?

A professionally measured residential set is typically accurate to within 1/4 inch to 1/2 inch on room dimensions, tight enough for design, cabinetry planning, and contractor pricing. Laser scanned sets can be tighter, often within 1/8 inch. No set is perfect, because old walls are rarely perfectly square or plumb, and a good drafter notes those irregularities rather than hiding them.

### Can I measure my own home and have it drafted remotely?

Yes, for straightforward properties, if you are careful. You will need wall lengths, wall thicknesses at doorways, ceiling heights, window and door sizes and locations, and clear photos of each room. Accuracy depends entirely on your measurements, so double check diagonals before sending them.

### Do as-built drawings replace a land survey?

No. As-built drawings document the building itself. A land survey documents the lot, property lines, setbacks, and easements. Some projects need both, especially additions near a property line.

### How long are as-built drawings valid?

They stay valid until the building changes. A remodel, an addition, or a moved wall makes the affected sheets out of date, so remeasure before the next major project.

### What file formats should I ask for?

Ask for PDF plans for sharing and printing, plus DWG or DXF CAD files for future design work. If you also want a 3D model, ask whether the firm can deliver one from the same measurements, since a [3D model](/services/3d-modeling/) built on verified dimensions is far more useful than one built on guesses.
