A beautiful kitchen that is hard to cook in is a failed design. The kitchens owners love years later are the ones where the layout quietly does its job: the fridge, sink, and stove sit within easy reach, two people can pass without bumping, and everything has a place.

This guide covers the five proven kitchen layouts, the real numbers professionals design to, storage and lighting rules, and the mistakes that make kitchens frustrating.

## Start with the work triangle, not the finishes

Before choosing a single cabinet door, map the work triangle: the path between the sink, the cooktop, and the refrigerator. This is the most-used route in any kitchen, and getting it right is the single biggest predictor of a kitchen that feels good to use.

The classic rules, still taught in kitchen design today:

- Each leg of the triangle should be between 4 and 9 feet
- The total of all three legs should be between 13 and 26 feet
- No leg should cross an island, peninsula, or major traffic path

A triangle that is too small means spinning between appliances with no landing space; one that is too large means miles of walking to make dinner. Before you finalize anything, draw the triangle on your [floor plan](/services/2d-floor-plans/). If it breaks these rules, the layout needs rethinking before finishes get chosen.

## The 5 kitchen layouts that work

Every functional kitchen is a variation of one of these five layouts. Pick the one that fits your space.

### 1. Galley kitchen

Two parallel runs of cabinets with a corridor between them. The most efficient layout for small spaces because every step lands you at a work surface.

- **Best for:** narrow rooms, apartments, one-cook households
- **Key number:** keep the aisle 42 to 48 inches wide (36 inches minimum for a single cook)
- **Watch out for:** dead-end galleys trap heat and people; leave the end open to the living area if possible

A well-lit galley with full-height cabinets on one wall is a cooking powerhouse. The compactness is the feature.

### 2. L-shaped kitchen

Cabinets run along two perpendicular walls, forming an L. Open on two sides, so it connects easily to dining or living areas.

- **Best for:** small to medium kitchens, open-plan living, corner use
- **Key number:** keep walkways around the L at 36 to 42 inches; place the sink and cooktop on different arms to stretch the work triangle
- **Watch out for:** corner cabinets become dead space without pull-out hardware; budget $200 to $500 extra for corner solutions

The L is the most popular residential layout in the US for good reason: it is flexible, budget-friendly, and leaves room open for a table or an island.

### 3. U-shaped kitchen

Cabinets on three walls. The layout that puts the most storage and counter space within arm's reach.

- **Best for:** serious cooks, larger rooms, people who want maximum storage
- **Key number:** the room needs at least 10 by 10 feet for a comfortable U; opposite runs should sit 5 to 7 feet apart
- **Watch out for:** the U can feel enclosed, so use lighter upper cabinets or open shelving on one arm

If cooking is your main hobby, nothing beats the U. Everything is within a step or two, and the third wall turns into deep storage or a bank of tall appliances.

### 4. Island kitchen

Any layout (usually L or U) plus a freestanding island in the center. The island adds prep space, seating, and often a second sink or cooktop.

- **Best for:** rooms at least 12 by 12 feet, families, entertaining
- **Key numbers:** 42 inches of clearance on every side of the island (48 inches if two cooks); seating needs 24 inches of width per person

Islands are the most requested kitchen feature, but they fail when the room is too small. Measure the clearances honestly before committing.

### 5. Open-plan / great room kitchen

The kitchen shares space with the dining and living areas, usually with a large island or peninsula as the divider.

- **Best for:** entertaining, family life, modern homes
- **Key number:** keep the cooking zone at least 4 feet clear of the main traffic lane to the living area
- **Watch out for:** noise and cooking smells travel; invest in a proper range hood (at least 400 CFM for a standard 30-inch cooktop)

Open plans are wonderful for togetherness. The fix for noise and cooking smells is zoning: define the kitchen with the island, change the flooring or ceiling height slightly, and plan storage so the counter can be cleared fast when guests arrive.

## The real numbers: clearances that matter

Professionals design kitchens to a handful of numbers. Get these right and the kitchen will feel comfortable no matter which layout you choose.

- **Countertop height:** 36 inches standard. Taller cooks (over 6'2") should consider 38 to 39 inches; baking stations often drop to 30 to 34 inches for leverage.
- **Countertop depth:** 25 to 26 inches, which fits a 24-inch cabinet plus a slight overhang.
- **Walkway (one cook):** 36 inches minimum
- **Walkway (two cooks or cook + traffic):** 42 to 48 inches
- **Work aisle (opposite counters):** 42 inches minimum, 48 inches comfortable
- **Dishwasher to sink:** place them within 36 inches of each other, ideally side by side
- **Fridge door clearance:** leave at least 2 inches beside a wall so the door opens fully; allow 48 to 50 inches in front for an open door
- **Range hood:** mount 30 to 36 inches above the cooktop for gas, 24 to 30 inches for electric
- **Countertop space beside the cooktop:** at least 15 inches on one side, 12 inches on the other, as landing zones for hot pans
- **Trash placement:** within 36 inches of the sink, because that is where prep waste happens

Print these numbers out and check your plan against every one of them. Layouts that ignore clearances are the number one source of kitchen regret.

## Storage: plan it by zone, not by cabinet count

A kitchen with plenty of cabinets but nothing near the dishwasher still feels short on storage. Plan storage where the work happens: cooking tools and spices near the stove (deep drawers beat lower cabinets here), knives and cutting boards near the sink, daily dishes by the dishwasher, and groceries near the fridge. One dedicated cabinet for counter gadgets clears visual clutter instantly.

## Lighting: three layers, not one

A single ceiling light leaves shadows where you chop food. You need task lighting (under-cabinet strips over the counters, roughly $15 to $30 per foot installed), ambient lighting (ceiling or recessed lights, spaced 4 to 6 feet apart), and decorative pendants over the island (hung 30 to 36 inches above the surface). Put the layers on separate switches or dimmers.

## 5 kitchen layout mistakes to avoid

1. **Blocking the triangle with an island.** If the island sits between the sink and the stove, the workflow is broken. Offset it or shrink it.
2. **No landing space beside appliances.** Hot pans and full sheet trays need somewhere to land within arm's reach of every appliance.
3. **Fridge in the far corner.** The fridge is the most-visited appliance. Put it on the outer edge so snackers do not walk through the cook's zone.
4. **Too little counter where it counts.** You need 36 inches of continuous prep counter, ideally next to the sink. Small broken-up counters add up in inches but are useless for real prep.
5. **No electrical plan.** Add at least one outlet every 4 feet of counter (US code), plus dedicated circuits for the microwave, dishwasher, disposal, and fridge. Retrofitting costs far more than adding them during the build.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best kitchen layout for a small kitchen?

The galley is the most efficient for small kitchens because it concentrates all work surfaces within a few steps. An L-shaped layout is the best runner-up when you want some openness. Skip the island unless the room genuinely has 42 to 48 inches of clearance to spare on all sides.

### How much does it cost to design a kitchen layout?

Professional kitchen design typically runs $2,500 to $8,000 for the design alone (cabinets and construction extra) in 2026. Remote design services charge $400 to $1,000 per room and include [3D renders](/services/3d-rendering/) of the finished layout, so you can verify clearances and sightlines before spending on cabinetry.

### Should the sink go under the window?

It is the classic placement and it works well: natural light over the prep and cleanup zone. The only real constraint is plumbing. Moving a sink to an exterior wall can cost $500 to $2,000 if the drain stack is far away. If the window is on the wrong wall for your triangle, put the sink where the workflow needs it instead.

### Is the kitchen work triangle outdated?

Not outdated, just evolved. Designers now talk about work zones (prep, cooking, cleanup) instead of a strict triangle, but the rules underneath are the same: keep the sink, stove, and fridge within easy walking distance and free of obstacles. Any good layout passes both tests.

### How big should a kitchen island be?

Minimum useful size is about 36 by 60 inches; a comfortable island with seating is 48 by 72 inches or larger. More important is clearance around it: 42 inches on every side for one cook, 48 inches when two people cook. A room under 12 by 12 feet usually cannot fit a proper island.

### Can I change my kitchen layout, or am I stuck with the existing plumbing?

You can change it, but plumbing moves are the expensive part. Moving a sink or dishwasher within about 4 feet of the existing drain usually costs $500 to $1,500; relocating it across the room can run $2,000 to $5,000. Get a plumber's quote before committing to a new layout, because plumbing cost often decides which layout is practical.
