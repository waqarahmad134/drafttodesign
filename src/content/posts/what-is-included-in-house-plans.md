When a builder quotes your project or a permit office asks for your plans, they are not asking for a single drawing. A full set of house plans is a package of coordinated sheets, each one answering a different question: where the house sits on the lot, how big each room is, what the outside looks like, how the structure holds together, and where the wiring and plumbing run. If any sheet is missing, someone on site has to guess, and guessing is where costly mistakes start.

This guide walks through a typical residential plan set sheet by sheet, so you know exactly what you should receive, what to check on each page, and what is usually not included.

## What a full set of house plans actually is

A plan set, also called a construction drawing set, is the instruction manual for building your home. The sheets are drawn to scale, dimensioned in feet and inches, and cross-referenced to each other, so a detail on one sheet matches the same detail on another. Most US homes need between 15 and 30 sheets, depending on size and complexity. A small single-story home may need around 15 sheets, while a large two-story home with a basement can reach 30 or more.

Every sheet carries a title block in the corner with the project name, sheet number, scale, date, and revision number. Always confirm you are working from the latest revision before approving anything or sending the set to a contractor.

## The cover sheet and general notes

The first sheet is the cover sheet. It usually shows a rendered view or elevation of the home, the project address, the client name, the designer's contact information, and a sheet index listing every sheet in the set with its title. Before anything else, read the index and check that every sheet listed is actually present.

The general notes sheet, sometimes combined with the cover, lists the building codes the design follows, the design loads the structure is engineered for, standard material specifications, and abbreviations used throughout the set. These notes are legally part of the drawings, so do not skip them.

## The site plan

The site plan shows your house on its lot, viewed from above. It marks the property lines, setbacks from each boundary, the driveway, walkways, patios, and the position of the house relative to the street. It also shows grading, drainage direction, utility connection points, and sometimes existing trees that must be protected.

Check the setbacks carefully. If the house sits closer to a property line than your local zoning allows, the permit will be rejected. ## The foundation plan

The foundation plan shows the footprint of the house at ground level, including footings, foundation walls, piers, and the slab or crawl space layout. It gives footing widths and depths, slab thickness, reinforcement details, and the locations of plumbing rough-ins that pass through the slab.

Check that the foundation outline matches the floor plan above it, and that openings for plumbing and mechanical runs are marked. If you are building on expansive clay soils, common in parts of Texas, the foundation type and reinforcement will differ from a standard slab, and that should be visible here.

## Floor plans

Floor plans are the sheets most homeowners study first, and for good reason. There is one plan per level, including the basement if there is one. Each shows the room layout, wall thicknesses, door and window locations with sizes, stairways, built-in features such as fireplaces and kitchen counters, and room dimensions.

Read these sheets room by room and imagine daily life in them. Check that furniture fits, that door swings do not clash with fixtures, that hallways are at least 36 inches wide, and that every bedroom has a legal egress window. Dimensions are written in feet and inches, so 12-6 means 12 feet 6 inches. Trust the written dimensions, not measurements taken off a printed page, because printers routinely resize drawings. If you are starting from scratch, a professional [2D floor plan](/services/2d-floor-plans/) is the usual first deliverable.

## Elevations

Elevations show the outside of the house from each side: front, rear, left, and right. They are flat views, not perspective drawings, and they show exterior wall heights, roof pitch, window and door placement, exterior materials and finishes, trim details, and grade lines where the ground meets the walls.

Compare the elevations against the floor plans. A window shown on a floor plan should appear in the same position on the matching elevation. ## Building sections

Sections are vertical cuts through the house, as if you sliced it open and looked at the inside of the wall assembly. A typical set includes at least one longitudinal and one cross section. These sheets show floor-to-ceiling heights, floor assembly thickness, roof structure, stair construction, and how each level connects.

Sections are where ceiling heights get verified. A plan might list 9-foot ceilings, but the section proves it by showing the actual distance between finished floor and finished ceiling, including the space taken by joists and ductwork.

## Roof plan and framing plans

The roof plan shows the roof from above: its shape, slopes, ridges, valleys, hips, and the locations of vents, chimneys, and skylights. Framing plans show the structural skeleton: floor joists, wall framing, beams, headers over openings, and roof trusses or rafters, with member sizes and spacing noted.

Check that the roof plan matches the elevations, so the roof you see from the street is the roof that gets built, and that large open spans, such as a wide great room, have beams sized for them. In many states, the structural sheets must be stamped by a licensed structural engineer before a permit is issued, which is a separate step from the design drawings.

## Electrical, plumbing, and mechanical sheets

These are often called the MEP sheets, and they are sometimes split into separate plans.

The electrical plan shows outlet, switch, and light fixture locations, ceiling fan boxes, dedicated circuits for appliances, panel location, and sometimes low-voltage points for internet and security. Walk through it room by room and count outlets against how you actually live.

The plumbing plan shows water supply and drain lines, fixture locations, pipe sizes, and venting. It is usually drawn over the floor plan so you can see exactly which wall each pipe runs through.

The mechanical plan covers heating, cooling, and ventilation: equipment locations, duct runs, vent locations, and return air paths.

## Details and schedules

Detail sheets zoom in on parts of the house that need precise construction, such as stair sections, fireplace construction, window and door flashing, deck connections, and foundation-to-wall transitions. These are drawn at a larger scale, often 1/2 inch or 3/4 inch equals 1 foot, so the builder can see exactly how layers come together.

Schedules are tables that list every window and door in the house with its size, type, and material, plus finish schedules for flooring, paint, and fixtures where included. When you receive the set, check that the window schedule count matches the windows drawn on the plans.

## What is usually NOT included

A standard plan set does not automatically include everything you might expect, so clarify these before you order:

- A structural engineer's stamp, which many jurisdictions require separately.
- A land survey or soil report, which come from surveyors and geotechnical firms.
- Interior design selections such as paint colors, tile patterns, and furniture.
- Landscape design beyond the basic site plan, which is a separate [exterior design and landscaping](/services/exterior-design-landscaping/) service.
- Permit fees, which are paid to your local building department.

Knowing these exclusions up front prevents the surprise of an extra invoice halfway through the permit process.

## How to review your plan set before approving it

Work through the set in this order: confirm nothing is missing from the sheet index, check that site plan setbacks and the foundation outline match the floor plans, compare elevations against floor plans, verify ceiling heights on the sections, walk the electrical plan room by room, and confirm the schedules match the drawings. Flag anything that does not line up and get a revised sheet before construction starts. Revisions on paper are cheap; revisions in concrete are not.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many sheets are in a full set of house plans?

A typical US home needs 15 to 30 sheets. Small single-story homes sit at the low end, while larger two-story homes with basements, complex roofs, or detailed MEP layouts sit at the high end. The sheet index on the cover sheet tells you exactly how many sheets your set contains.

### Can I build a house from just a floor plan?

No. A floor plan alone does not show wall heights, roof structure, foundation details, or electrical and plumbing layouts. A builder can give a rough estimate from a floor plan, but a permit office will require the full coordinated set, and building without it risks structural and code problems.

### Do house plans need an architect's stamp?

It depends on your state and local jurisdiction. Many areas allow residential plans prepared by a qualified designer or [architectural drafting](/services/architectural-drafting/) service, while others require a licensed architect or engineer stamp for certain sizes or structural systems. Check with your local building department before ordering.

### How long does it take to produce a full plan set?

For a typical custom home, expect 3 to 6 weeks from approved floor plan to a complete drawing set. Pre-drawn plans can be delivered in days but usually need modifications for your lot and local codes.

### What should I do if I find an error in my plans?

Send the discrepancy to your designer in writing, referencing the sheet number and revision, and ask for a corrected sheet. Never let the builder improvise a fix on site without a revised drawing, because the change may affect structure, code compliance, or another sheet you have not checked.

A complete plan set is your best protection against surprises during construction. Every sheet answers a question someone on site will ask, and the cheapest time to answer it is on paper. If you are planning a custom home, an addition, or a remodel, start with a clear floor plan and build the full set from there.
