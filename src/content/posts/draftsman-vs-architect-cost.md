If you are planning a remodel, an addition, or a new build, one of the first decisions you face is who should draw the plans: a draftsman or an architect. Hire the wrong one and you either overpay for services you did not need, or end up with drawings your builder or permit office cannot use. Either mistake costs real money. This guide breaks down the roles, the real 2026 cost ranges, and a clear rule of thumb for which one your project needs.

## The short answer

- **Hire a draftsman** when you mainly need professional drawings: floor plans, elevations, sections, as-builts, and contractor-ready documentation. Typical cost: **$50 to $150 per hour**, or **$1,000 to $5,000** for a complete residential drawing set depending on scope.
- **Hire an architect** when you need original building design, structural decisions, permit-stamped drawings, or coordination with engineers. Typical cost: **$125 to $400+ per hour**, or **5% to 15% of construction cost**, which can mean **$25,000 to $75,000+** in fees on a mid-range custom home.

Most ordinary residential projects do not need an architect's full service. Read on to see where your project falls.

## What a draftsman actually does

A draftsman (also called a drafting technician or design drafter) is a trained professional who turns ideas into precise technical drawings using CAD software. A good residential draftsman produces:

- **Floor plans** with exact dimensions, wall thicknesses, door and window schedules
- **Elevations** showing what each side of the building looks like
- **Sections and details** that show builders how things go together
- **As-built drawings** documenting an existing structure as it stands today
- **Electrical, reflected ceiling, and demolition plans** for remodels

Most draftsmen work from your sketches, measurements, photos, or site visits. They are specialists in documentation: they take a design direction and turn it into the clean, dimensioned sheets a contractor can price and build from. What they typically do **not** do: make structural engineering decisions or stamp drawings for permit submission (licensing rules vary by state).

## What an architect actually does

An architect is a licensed professional who has completed an accredited degree, years of supervised experience, and a licensing exam. The license is the key difference: in most states, only a licensed architect (or engineer) can **stamp and seal** drawings, and many building departments require that stamp for permits on new construction, structural work, and commercial projects.

Beyond the stamp, architects are trained as designers, not just drawers. For a custom home they typically work through schematic design, design development, construction documents, and construction administration, staying involved for months. That full process is genuinely valuable for complex projects, and it is what makes architects expensive: you are paying for design expertise, liability, and months of involvement, not just drawings.

## Draftsman vs architect: the key differences side by side

| | Draftsman | Architect |
|---|---|---|
| **License** | Not licensed as an architect; certifications optional | Licensed by the state; legally required for certain work |
| **Core skill** | Technical drawings and documentation | Building design, plus drawings |
| **Permit stamping** | Usually not permitted to stamp | Can stamp and seal permit sets |
| **Typical residential role** | Plans for remodels, additions, as-builts, space planning | Custom homes, structural work, complex sites |
| **Involvement** | Focused engagement: drawings delivered, done | Often months-long, through construction |
| **2026 cost range** | $50-$150/hr or $1,000-$5,000 per drawing set | $125-$400+/hr or 5%-15% of build cost |

The right choice depends entirely on your project's complexity and your local permit rules.

## How much each costs in 2026: real numbers

### Draftsman pricing

- **Hourly:** $50 to $150 per hour. Freelance and remote draftsmen often land in the $40 to $100 range; established local firms charge more. Simple jobs like redrawing a floor plan from your sketch might take 4 to 10 hours, so $200 to $1,000 all-in.
- **Per square foot:** $0.50 to $2.50 per square foot of designed space for full plan sets. A 2,500 sq ft home design with floor plans, elevations, sections, and details might run $1,500 to $6,000.
- **Flat project fees:** most residential drafting work is quoted flat once the scope is clear. Expect roughly $800 to $2,000 for a single-room remodel set, $1,500 to $4,000 for a whole-house as-built or renovation set, and $3,000 to $7,500 for a complete new-home drawing package.
- **As-built surveys:** $0.25 to $1.00 per square foot, or $500 to $2,500 for a typical single-family home.

Remote drafting services have pushed the low end down further, since the work is digital and location-independent. If your project does not need a site visit, remote quotes are one of the easiest ways to save.

### Architect pricing

- **Hourly:** $125 to $400+ per hour, with principals at well-known firms exceeding $500.
- **Percentage of construction:** the traditional model, 5% to 15% of total construction cost for full service. On a $500,000 custom home build, that is $25,000 to $75,000 in architectural fees.
- **Fixed fee:** common for defined residential projects. A straightforward addition or remodel design might be quoted at $5,000 to $25,000; a full custom home commonly $20,000 to $100,000+.

The takeaway: an architect typically costs **5 to 10 times** what a draftsman charges for documentation, because you are buying a different service. When you genuinely need it, it is worth it.

## When to hire a draftsman

A draftsman is the right call when the design direction is essentially settled and you need it translated into professional, buildable drawings. Typical cases:

- **Kitchen or bathroom remodels** where you know the layout you want and need dimensioned plans for the contractor and cabinet maker
- **As-built documentation** of your existing home before a renovation, so everyone works from accurate drawings
- **Space planning and furniture layouts** for a new home or office
- **Garage conversions, basement finishes, and simple additions** where the structure is straightforward and your jurisdiction does not require a stamp
- **Real estate marketing floor plans**, including 3D visuals to sell or rent a property

If your total budget for design documentation is under $5,000, a draftsman is almost certainly the right professional. ## When to hire an architect

Bring in an architect when the project involves real design risk, legal requirements, or complexity that goes beyond documentation:

- **A new custom home**, especially on a difficult lot (slope, floodplain, tight setbacks, strict HOA or design review)
- **Structural changes:** removing load-bearing walls, adding a second story, major additions, or anything that changes how the building stands up
- **Permit-stamped drawings:** if your building department requires an architect's or engineer's seal, no draftsman can substitute for it, so confirm this with your local permit office first
- **Commercial projects**, where most jurisdictions legally require a licensed architect
- **Design from scratch with no clear direction:** if you have a lot and a vague idea, an architect's design process earns its fee
- **Projects needing engineering coordination:** architects routinely coordinate structural, mechanical, and civil engineers, which matters on complex builds

Mixed approaches are common and legitimate: many homeowners hire an architect for the design phases and a draftsman for later documentation tweaks, or have a draftsman produce drawings that an engineer reviews and stamps.

## The gray area: projects where either can work

Some projects sit in the middle, and the deciding factor is usually your local building department's rules:

- **Home additions:** a draftsman can draw the full set; many jurisdictions accept unstamped drawings for one- and two-family residential work under certain size limits, while others want an engineer's review of the structural sheets.
- **ADUs and garage conversions:** the design is usually simple, so a draftsman plus a structural engineer's letter (a few hundred dollars) often covers everything the city asks for.
- **Whole-house renovations:** if no walls move structurally, a draftsman's set is typically all the contractor and the city need.

When in doubt, ask your building department what they require **before** you hire anyone. That five-minute call can save you from paying for drawings you cannot use, or from overpaying for a stamp you never needed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a draftsman really that much cheaper than an architect?

Yes. A draftsman sells focused drawing work, typically $50 to $150 per hour. An architect sells licensed design responsibility, typically $125 to $400+ per hour or a percentage of construction. For documentation-only work, expect the architect to cost roughly five to ten times more for an equivalent drawing set.

### Can a draftsman draw plans that get a building permit?

Often, yes, for residential work. Many US jurisdictions accept permit drawings for single-family homes, remodels, and additions without an architect's stamp, as long as the drawings are complete and code-compliant. Rules vary by city and county, and structural work usually needs an engineer's review regardless. Confirm with your local building department before commissioning drawings.

### Do I need an architect to remodel my kitchen or bathroom?

Almost never, unless you are moving load-bearing walls. A kitchen or bath remodel is documentation work: dimensioned floor plans, elevations for the cabinet maker, and electrical and plumbing layouts for the trades. A draftsman handles all of this, typically for $800 to $2,000 for the full drawing set.

### How do I find a good draftsman?

Look for a portfolio of residential work similar to yours, clear flat-fee quotes, and CAD proficiency (Revit or AutoCAD for technical sets). Ask how they handle revisions and what file formats you receive (PDF plus editable DWG is ideal). Remote drafting services can be excellent value for projects that do not need a site visit.

### What if I start with a draftsman and realize I need an architect?

This happens, and it is not a disaster. Clean, well-organized drawings from a draftsman give an architect a head start, which can reduce the architect's billable hours. The reverse is rarely true: paying an architect for work a draftsman could have done is money you will not get back.

## The bottom line

Use this rule of thumb: if your project is structurally simple, the design direction is clear, and your building department does not require a licensed stamp, hire a draftsman and keep thousands of dollars in your pocket. If you need original design, structural decisions, or a stamp for permits, hire an architect and consider the fee money well spent.
