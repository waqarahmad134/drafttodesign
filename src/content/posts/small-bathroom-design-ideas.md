A small bathroom is usually the hardest-working room in the house. The typical American guest bath measures about 5 by 7 feet, just 35 square feet, yet it has to fit a toilet, sink, shower or tub, storage, and a door that swings without hitting anything. The good news is that small bathrooms respond better to good design than almost any other room. A few smart choices can make a cramped 5x7 feel genuinely comfortable.

## Start with the layout, not the decor

Most small bathrooms feel cramped because of the floor plan, not the finishes. Before you pick tile or paint, look at how the room is arranged. Three layout moves make the biggest difference:

**Swap the swing door for a pocket door or barn door.** A standard 30-inch door needs about 7 square feet of clear swing space inside the room. In a 35-square-foot bathroom, that is 20 percent of your floor gone. A pocket door slides into the wall and gives that space back. Budget roughly $300 to $600 extra for the pocket-door frame and installation during a remodel.

**Respect the clearances.** Building code requires at least 21 inches of clear space in front of a toilet, and 15 inches from the center of the toilet to any side wall. Showers need a minimum 30 by 30 inches of clear floor area. These are minimums, not comfort targets. If your layout squeezes fixtures tighter than this, the room will feel wrong no matter how pretty the tile is.

**Keep the sight line open.** When you open the door, you should see across the room to a window, a mirror, or the far wall, not the side of a toilet. It costs nothing to plan and is expensive to fix later.

If your bathroom layout is fighting you, a professional [interior design](/services/interior-design/) plan can test several arrangements on paper before any demolition starts. Moving a toilet drain 2 feet during planning costs a fraction of moving it after the tile is set.

## Choose fixtures sized for small bathrooms

Standard fixtures are designed for average rooms. In a small bath, every inch counts, and compact fixtures buy you real space.

**Wall-hung toilets** save 6 to 9 inches of floor depth compared to a standard 28-inch-deep toilet, and the open floor underneath makes the room read larger. They cost more ($500 to $1,200 for the bowl plus in-wall carrier versus $200 to $400 for a standard toilet), but in a tight layout the extra inches are worth it.

**Pedestal, wall-mount, or corner sinks** free up floor space that a bulky vanity cabinet eats. A 24-inch vanity is standard; an 18-inch-deep compact vanity or a wall-mount sink can save 6 inches of walkway. If you need storage, a recessed medicine cabinet above the sink (see below) replaces much of what a vanity cabinet holds.

**Curbless or low-threshold showers** replace the tub in most small-bath remodels. A 32 by 48-inch shower with a frameless glass panel feels dramatically larger than the same footprint with a shower curtain, because your eye travels straight through to the far wall. A tub needs at least 60 by 30 inches, so keeping one in a 5x7 bath usually means sacrificing everything else.

## Color, tile, and the visual tricks that work

Light colors make walls recede. That advice is everywhere because it is true, but the details matter more than the rule.

**Keep the palette to two or three colors.** Every new color or material in a small room is a visual "event" that chops the space into pieces. One wall tile, one floor tile, one paint color, and one metal finish is plenty.

**Use large-format tile.** It seems backward, but 12 by 24-inch tiles make a small bathroom look bigger than 4-inch mosaics do. Fewer grout lines mean fewer visual interruptions. Match the grout color closely to the tile so the surface reads as one continuous plane. A high-contrast grout grid in a tiny room looks busy and smaller.

**Run the floor tile straight into the shower.** When the shower floor and the bathroom floor are the same tile, the room reads as one larger space instead of two small ones. This is one of the most effective small-bath tricks designers use.

**Paint with high light reflectance.** Look for paint with a Light Reflectance Value (LRV) above 70 for walls in a windowless bath. Soft whites, pale warm grays, and very light sage all work. Save deep colors for one accent, like the vanity, and only if the room has good light.

## Light it like a bigger room

Bad lighting is why so many small bathrooms feel like closets. One harsh overhead light creates shadows in every corner, and shadows read as walls closing in.

**Layer three light sources.** Aim for an overhead light (a flush-mount or two 4-inch recessed cans), task lighting at the mirror (sconces at eye level on both sides beat a single bar above the mirror), and ideally some natural light. Total target: roughly 75 to 100 foot-candles at the vanity, which usually means 1,500 to 2,500 lumens in a 35-square-foot room.

**Choose 3000K color temperature.** It is warm enough to flatter skin and bright enough to feel clean. Avoid 5000K "daylight" bulbs in a small bath; they make tile look clinical.

**Borrow light with glass.** A frosted-glass door or a transom window above the door pulls hallway light into a windowless bath. A solar tube, typically $500 to $1,500 installed, can transform a dark interior bathroom.

## Storage without the clutter

Small bathrooms do not lack storage, they lack *hidden* storage. Everything visible in a 35-square-foot room competes for attention.

**Recess instead of projecting.** A medicine cabinet recessed into the wall gives 4 inches of deep storage with zero intrusion into the room. Shower niches recessed between studs hold bottles without a caddy hanging off the wall. A hamper or linen cabinet built into an unused wall cavity beats any freestanding shelf.

**Go vertical.** The wall above the toilet is usually dead space. A recessed or shallow cabinet there (no deeper than 8 inches, so nobody hits their head) adds real storage. Tall, narrow linen towers, 12 to 15 inches wide, fit beside most vanities.

## Mirrors and glass: the space multipliers

A mirror does not just reflect light, it visually doubles whatever it faces. In a small bathroom, that is free square footage.

**Run the mirror wall to wall above the vanity.** A 36 or 48-inch mirror spanning the full vanity width reflects the whole room, not just your face. Frameless or thin-framed mirrors keep the edge from drawing a hard line.

**Use clear glass for the shower enclosure.** Frameless clear glass disappears; frosted glass or a curtain stops the eye and shrinks the room. A daily squeegee takes 20 seconds and handles water spots.

**Consider a mirrored medicine cabinet.** It combines two space multipliers, hidden storage and a full-width mirror, in one fixture.

## Do not forget ventilation

A small bathroom concentrates moisture fast. An undersized fan leads to peeling paint, moldy grout, and fogged mirrors that never clear.

**Size the fan to the room.** The rule of thumb is 1 CFM per square foot of bathroom, so a 35-square-foot bath needs at least a 35 CFM fan. Buy 50 to 80 CFM for real-world performance, and make sure it vents to the exterior, not into the attic. A fan with a humidity sensor or timer switch, about $30 to $60, runs itself and prevents the number-one small-bath maintenance headache.

## What a small bathroom remodel costs

Realistic 2026 numbers for a 5x7 bathroom in the US:

- **Cosmetic refresh** (paint, mirror, lighting, fixtures, no layout change): $3,000 to $7,000
- **Mid-range remodel** (new tile, vanity, toilet, shower, pocket door): $10,000 to $20,000
- **Full gut with layout change** (moving plumbing, curbless shower, wall-hung toilet): $18,000 to $30,000

Layout changes drive cost because moving a toilet or shower drain means opening the floor. That is exactly why getting the plan right first matters. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can you fit a tub in a small 5x7 bathroom?

It is possible but rarely advisable. A standard 60 by 30-inch tub consumes almost half the floor, leaving tight clearances everywhere else. If a tub is non-negotiable, consider a 54-inch apartment-size tub or a deep Japanese-style soaking tub with a smaller footprint, and pair it with a wall-hung toilet and compact sink to claw back space.

### Do dark colors ever work in a small bathroom?

Yes, but only with strong light. A deep navy or charcoal powder room with a big mirror and bright sconces can look dramatic and intentional. Dark walls need light floors, a light ceiling, and generous lighting. In a windowless bath with one bulb, dark paint will feel like a cave.

### How long does a small bathroom remodel take?

A cosmetic refresh takes 3 to 5 days. A mid-range remodel with new tile and fixtures typically runs 2 to 3 weeks. A full gut with plumbing relocation takes 3 to 5 weeks, plus lead time for materials. Tile and custom glass are the usual schedule drivers, so order them before demolition starts.

### Should I DIY a small bathroom remodel?

Paint, mirrors, lighting swaps, and accessory installation are good DIY projects. Leave plumbing moves, electrical work, waterproofing, and tile setting to licensed pros. Waterproofing mistakes in a shower do not show up for a year or two, and by then the repair costs far more than hiring it out.

### What is the cheapest way to make a small bathroom feel bigger?

Declutter, paint it a light color, swap the shower curtain for clear glass, and upgrade the lighting. Those four changes often cost under $1,000 total and deliver most of the visual gain. A bigger mirror and a pocket door add the most perceived space per dollar after that.
