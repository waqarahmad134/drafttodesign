A 3D rendering project goes one of two ways. Either you hand the studio a tidy package of drawings, references, and decisions, and the renders come back looking exactly like the building in your head, or the whole thing stalls in an email thread of clarifications, re-renders, and revision fees. The difference is almost never the artist. It is the preparation.

This checklist walks you through everything a rendering studio needs before the work starts, in the order that matters most. Spend an hour on this upfront and you will usually get better images, fewer revisions, and a faster turnaround. Skip it and you will pay for that hour several times over in delays.

## 1. Gather your drawings and dimensions

The single most important thing you can provide is accurate drawings. A 3D artist builds the model from these, so every missing dimension is a guess you will have to correct later.

**What to collect:**

- Floor plans with room labels and wall dimensions
- Elevations (front, rear, and both sides) for exterior projects
- A site plan or survey showing the lot, setbacks, and orientation
- Sections or roof plans if the roofline is complex
- Ceiling heights for every room in interior projects

Drawings do not need to be pretty. A hand-drawn sketch with clear dimensions works better than a beautiful but unmeasured plan. If you already have a [2D floor plan drawn professionally](/services/2d-floor-plans), you are ahead of the game, since the studio can build directly from it.

What if you have no drawings at all? That is fine, it just adds a step. Either have floor plans drawn first or tell the studio that modeling starts from your descriptions and rough sketches. Expect the quote and timeline to reflect the extra work. See our guide on [how long 3D rendering takes](/blog/how-long-3d-rendering-takes/) for realistic turnarounds at each level of preparation.

**Quick check:** Can someone who has never seen your project draw the basic building from what you are sending? If not, add more.

## 2. Collect reference images of the look you want

"Modern" means completely different things to different people. A handful of reference images removes the ambiguity in seconds.

**What to collect:**

- 5 to 10 photos of buildings, rooms, or materials in the style you want
- Screenshots of finishes you like: brick tones, siding colors, cabinet styles, countertop patterns
- Photos of details that matter to you: window styles, railing designs, front doors, light fixtures
- Examples of the mood: bright and airy, warm and cozy, dramatic dusk shots

Save the links or images in one folder or one email. Annotate the key ones with a sentence each: "This exact brick color," "This kind of porch railing," "Not this, too cold." That annotation takes five minutes and saves a full revision round, because the artist no longer has to guess which part of each image you liked.

## 3. Decide on your materials and finishes

This is the step most clients postpone, and it is the biggest cause of re-renders. The studio cannot render a kitchen until it knows whether the cabinets are white shaker or dark walnut.

**Decide these before kickoff:**

- **Exteriors:** siding or brick type and color, roof material and color, trim color, window and door colors, driveway and walkway materials
- **Interiors:** wall paint colors, flooring material per room, cabinet style and color, countertop material, backsplash tile, fixture finishes (brass, matte black, chrome)
- **Landscaping:** level of planting detail, lawn vs. xeriscaping, hardscape materials

You do not need final brand names and product codes for every item. Paint color names, material names ("white oak," "standing-seam metal roof"), and reference photos are enough. What you want to avoid is telling the studio "just pick something" and then asking for three color changes after the render is done. Most studios, including ours, include one or two revision rounds; changing materials after rendering burns through them fast.

## 4. List the views you need

Each camera angle is a separate image that needs lighting, framing, and post-processing. Decide the shot list upfront instead of ordering renders one at a time.

**A typical residential package looks like this:**

- 2 to 3 exterior views: front elevation, a dusk or evening shot, and a backyard or angle view
- 2 to 4 interior views: kitchen, primary living space, primary bedroom, and one more key room
- Optional: an aerial or street-level context view, a detail shot of a porch or entry

Also note the time of day and season for each view. A daytime render and a dusk render of the same house are different images with different lighting setups. If the project is for a listing, marketing brochure, or permit presentation, mention that too, because the framing and level of photorealism differ.

Budget tip: bundling all views of one project costs far less per image than ordering them individually across several rounds. See current [3D rendering pricing](/blog/3d-rendering-cost-2026/) to plan how many views your budget covers.

## 5. Prepare site context and surroundings

Renders look far more convincing when the building sits in a believable setting rather than floating in a white void.

**Provide what you can:**

- Photos of the actual lot, taken from several angles (a phone photo works)
- The address or coordinates so the artist can check lot shape and sun direction
- Notes on neighboring buildings: approximate height, color, and distance
- Whether you want the real surroundings modeled or a clean, neutral setting

For interior projects, the equivalent is context: tell the studio whether the room should look staged and lived-in (with furniture, decor, and small details) or clean and architectural. Staged interiors take longer to build but sell the space much better in listings and presentations.

## 6. Clarify people, cars, and seasonal details

Small details change the feel of a render dramatically, so decide them before rendering begins.

- **People:** Do you want people in the images? If so, how many, and doing what? (Walking up the path, sitting on the porch.) Some clients want empty images for a cleaner look.
- **Cars:** A car in the driveway adds scale and life to exterior views. Mention any preference.
- **Season and weather:** Lush summer landscaping, fall foliage, or bare winter trees all read very differently. Default to summer unless you say otherwise.
- **Sky and atmosphere:** Bright blue sky, soft overcast, or dramatic dusk. Dusk views are popular for exteriors because they photograph the warm interior glow, but they take more rendering time.

## 7. Set the timeline and the revision plan

Tell the studio your real deadline, including a buffer. If you need images for a listing that goes live on the 15th, say the 12th. Rush fees of 25 to 50 percent are standard when a project needs to jump the queue.

Then agree on the revision process:

- How many revision rounds are included in the quote (two is typical)
- Who on your side gives feedback (one decision-maker is ideal; feedback by committee produces contradictory notes)
- What a "revision" covers: adjusting colors and lighting, yes; changing the floor plan mid-project, that is new work

The single best habit for a smooth project: approve the untextured 3D model before rendering starts. When you sign off on the clay-gray model, the geometry is locked. Color and lighting changes after that point are cheap; rebuilding walls is not.

## Your pre-kickoff checklist

Copy this into your notes and tick each item off before you send the package:

1. Floor plans, elevations, and key dimensions collected
2. 5 to 10 reference images saved and annotated
3. Materials and finishes decided for every visible surface
4. Shot list written down with time of day for each view
5. Site photos and address provided
6. Decisions made on people, cars, season, and sky
7. Deadline communicated with a buffer
8. Revision rounds and single point of contact agreed

Send all of it in one organized package rather than in a drip of separate emails. Most studios can quote within 24 hours of receiving a complete package.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far in advance should I contact a rendering studio?

For a standard single-family home with good drawings in hand, allow two to three weeks from first contact to final images. The first few days usually go to quoting and gathering materials, modeling takes about a week, and rendering plus revisions take the rest. Start the conversation even earlier if your drawings are incomplete or the project is complex.

### Can I get renders if I only have rough sketches?

Yes. Rough sketches with clear dimensions are enough for an experienced studio to build an accurate model. The quote will be slightly higher than for a project with finished architectural drawings, because the artist spends more time interpreting and verifying measurements. What matters most is that the dimensions are consistent and complete.

### What file formats will I receive?

Typical deliverables are high-resolution JPEG or PNG images for each view, usually at 3000 to 5000 pixels wide, which works for print brochures and large displays. If you also need the raw [3D model](/services/3d-modeling), ask upfront: common formats include SKP, FBX, OBJ, and 3DS Max files. Confirm the formats before work starts, since converting between some formats can lose detail.

### How many revisions should I expect to need?

Most well-prepared projects need zero to two revision rounds. The first round usually covers small adjustments like paint shades, landscaping density, or lighting warmth. If you followed the checklist above, especially approving the untextured model and locking materials early, major rework is rare. Projects that skip preparation commonly need three or more rounds.

### Should the render match the real surroundings or look idealized?

It depends on the purpose. For permit boards and neighborhood presentations, an honest representation of the real lot and neighbors is best. For marketing, listings, and fundraising, a slightly idealized version with clean landscaping and a great sky is standard practice. Tell the studio which one you want so the artist models the right context.
